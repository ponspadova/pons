Elif Harput

University of Geneva, Geneva, Switzerland

Investigating Gray Matter Network Alterations in Preclinical Alzheimer's Disease Using Graph Theoretical Measures​

Read more

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the leading cause of dementia and represents a growing healthcare challenge with the increasing aging population. Pathological changes in the brain begin years before the onset of clinical symptoms and are characterized by accumulation of amyloid-beta (Aβ) plaques and neurofibrillary tau tangles follow by neuronal and synaptic degeneration. Alterations in gray matter networks have also been observed during the early stages of the disease and possibly associated with amyloid-beta deposition and tau pathology.

In this study, we investigated the relationship between alterations in gray matter network organization and AD-specific biomarkers in a preclinical AD cohort using graph theoretical measures.

Matteo Vitacca

University of Pavia, Pavia, Italy

Virtual Brain Modelling Reveals Altered Excitation/Inhibition Balance and Predicts Symptom Severity in Schizophrenia: Implications for Treatment-Resistant Schizophrenia

Read more

Schizophrenia (SZ) is a heterogeneous disorder characterized by positive, negative, cognitive and disorganisation symptoms, with distinct clinical subtypes and poorly understood aetiology. Traditionally, SZ has been ascribed to dopaminergic alterations, although accumulating evidence points to altered large-scale brain connectivity and dysregulation of glutamatergic and GABAergic transmission leading to disruption of excitation/inhibition (E/I) balance.

While quantifying these alterations would help clarify the neurophysiological basis of SZ and guide therapy, their identification in patients remains impractical. This is particularly relevant for treatment-resistant SZ (TRS), which affects a substantial proportion of patients and whose pathophysiology remains unclear.

Using virtual brain modeling, we simulated large-scale brain dynamics from (f)MRI data in 43 healthy controls (HC) and 33 medicated patients with chronic SZ , stratified into TRS and NON-TRS subgroups according to standardised criteria. We used the Wong-Wang neural mass model to derive four biophysically interpretable network parameters reflecting GABAergic and NMDA receptor-mediated glutamatergic transmission: global coupling (G), local inhibitory coupling (Ji), local excitatory coupling (JNMDA), and recurrent excitation (w+). We performed simulations both in the whole-brain and in resting-state networks (RSNs). Model parameters were compared between groups and tested against positive, negative, disorganisation and general symptoms severity as measured by the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS).

Compared to NON-TRS (n=17; age 39.2 years; 8F/9M), TRS (n=16; age 37.4 years; in 3F/13M) showed increased JNMDA (p = .043) and no no w+ (p = .048) at the whole-brain level, increased JNMDA (p = .017) and G (p = .025) in the dorsal attention network, lower G (p = .047) in the limbic network and increased w+ in the visual network. Across the clinical sample, Ji in the default mode network predicted negative symptom severity (R2 = 0.35, p = .001) while JNMDA in the limbic network and ventral attention network predicted total PANSS scores (R² = .37; p = .0006) and disorganisation severity (R² = .49; p < .0001).

These findings suggest that TRS is characterized by network-specific alterations in E/I balance, and support the use of subject-specific multiscale modelling to identify candidate biomarkers of symptom dimensions.

Enrico Maria Guarnuto​

Center for Mind/Brain Sciences - CIMeC, University of Trento, Trento, Italy

Some Connections Matter More than Others: What Topographic Neural Networks Model, and What They Leave Out

Read more

Category-selective regions have shaped decades of thinking about the visual brain, yet the rules governing their spatial arrangement have stayed elusive. A new generation of topographic neural networks offers a bold answer: cortical organization emerges when a model is pushed to keep its connections short, not just to perform well. Trained this way, these networks spontaneously develop map-like structures that echo real visual cortex, orientation maps, category-selective patches, the works. But look closer at what kind of connections these models actually have, and the picture gets more complicated. Some wiring is modeled; other kinds, lateral, recurrent, cross-area, are simply assumed away.

This lecture uses topographic neural networks as a case study to ask a broader question: when we explain brain organization through connections, which connections are we really talking about, and which ones are we quietly leaving out?

Leonardo Zaggia​

Carl von Ossietzky Universität Oldenburg, Oldenburg, Germany

Beyond the Manifest: Latent Structure and Better Measurement in Early Neurodevelopment

Read more

The data we collect on the developing brain is richer than the numbers we usually extract from it. Functional connectivity is compressed into static whole-brain graph metrics; infant cognition is compressed into a composite score.

In this talk I will argue that the interesting structure is latent rather than manifest, and present three approaches to reach it: two that recover hidden structure from existing data, and one that redesigns measurement itself.

First, on the brain side, I will present an application of Nested Spectral Partitioning to resting-state fMRI of preterm and full-term neonates from the developing Human Connectome Project. NSP describes the connectome as a nested hierarchy of modules and tracks how the brain moves between globally integrated and locally segregated states over time. Preterm neonates show a shifted balance and altered dynamics relative to term-born infants, differences that static metrics miss or misattribute.

Second, on the behavioural side, I will show how language-model embeddings of item text, anchored to an expert taxonomy, decompose the Bayley-III cognition composite into working memory, goal-directed problem solving and flexibility/shift, revealing domain-specific associations with perinatal risk and later behaviour that the single score attenuates or masks.

Finally, I will present an ongoing fNIRS study that moves beyond both resting-state recordings and examiner-scored behavioural tests: an eye-tracking controlled McGurk paradigm measuring audiovisual integration in six-month-old preterm and full-term infants, where gaze itself serves as an objective behavioural biomarker alongside cortical responses to congruent and incongruent speech.

Together, these projects illustrate how modern computational and measurement approaches let us extract more developmental signal from infant data, manifest and latent alike.

Workshops

Iván González-García

Jaume I University, Castellón de la Plana, Spain

From Task to Networks: A Practical Workshop on gPPI Analysis and Network-Based Statistics

Read more

Generalized Psychophysiological Interaction (gPPI) analysis is a framework for investigating task-dependent changes in functional connectivity (FC) between pairs of brain regions. In this workshop, participants will learn how to implement a complete gPPI analysis using task-related fMRI data, from data extraction and model specification to statistical analysis and visualization. Specifically, we will cover the construction of psychological, physiological, and interaction regressors, as well as the parameters required for a gPPI analysis. We will then compute FC matrices and explore different approaches to statistical inference and multiple-comparison correction, including False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction and parametric Network-Based Statistics (NBS).

Along the way, we will discuss key concepts related to FC analysis, including weighted vs. unweighted networks, directed vs. undirected FC, and regression- vs. correlation-based approaches to FC. We will also become familiar with commonly used network file formats and gain an overview of other relevant software tools for FC analysis and visualization.

No advanced programming experience is required, as participants will be provided with the necessary scripts and programming tools to perform the analyses, together with guidance to understand the main steps of the code and adapt it to their own research interests.

Riccardo Venturini

Scuola Superiore Studi Pavia - IUSS, Pavia, Italy

How Rich is the EEG signal? A Hands-On Introduction to Time-Resolved Decoding

Read more

What can we learn from brain activity beyond asking whether two experimental conditions differ? Modern predictive modelling offers a way to turn this question around: given a complex pattern of neural activity, can we recover the information being processed and track how that information changes from millisecond to millisecond?

In this proof-of-concept workshop, participants will be introduced to time-resolved decoding of EEG data through a concrete example from naturalistic language comprehension. Rather than relying on isolated stimuli and tightly controlled contrasts, we will work with EEG recorded while participants listened to continuous speech and with multiple properties annotated for every word. This provides a realistic methodological challenge: neural data are high-dimensional and noisy, while features of natural stimuli are often strongly correlated. Starting from the intuition behind encoding and decoding models, we will build a time-resolved predictive analysis and introduce back-to-back regression, a two-stage approach designed to combine the sensitivity of multivariate decoding with greater specificity about the contribution of correlated predictors. Participants will then apply these ideas in a hands-on session using real code and data, moving from neural signals and stimulus features to interpretable temporal decoding profiles.

Leonardo Zaggia

Carl von Ossietzky Universität Oldenburg, Oldenburg, Germany

From Words to Constructs: Analysing Questionnaire Items with Language-Model Embeddings

Read more

Psychological and neurodevelopmental tests are made of language: every item is a piece of text carrying assumptions about what it measures. Sentence-embedding models make this text computationally tractable by mapping items into a vector space where geometric proximity corresponds to semantic similarity. This opens a new, sample-independent window on old psychometric questions: Which items belong together? Do test constructs match their semantic structure? Where do two editions of a test diverge?

In this hands-on seminar, participants will build a complete embedding pipeline in Python using open-source tools and openly accessible items from psychological testing. Starting from raw item text, we will extract embeddings, compute pairwise similarities, compare them with the observed correlations between participants' responses, visualize the item space in two dimensions, and attempt to recover the test's construct structure without any response data, discussing where and why this fails. I will close by showing how this pipeline scales to a real application: decomposing the cognition scale of the Bayley-III, a widely used infant development test, into distinct cognitive domains.

No prior experience with language models is required; basic familiarity with Python is helpful. The session runs entirely in Google Colab, so no installation is needed. All materials will be openly available after the seminar.
